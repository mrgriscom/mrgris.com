--- kind: article created_at: 2026-01-23 title: "Mixing a DJ set in Reaper" --- I am the quintessential "press play" DJ. I don't have the skills to mix live. Nor would I really want to, even if I did have them. If I've done all the work of putting together a DJ set, I want to be enjoying it when it goes on, bouncing on the dancefloor and being not-fully-sober. I _don't_ want to be standing on the sidelines hunched over a computer, filled with the stress of doing a live performance. So for me, the ideal is to mix and pre-record your set in advance. Live DJ software (Rekordbox, Traktor, Serato, Mixxx, etc.) doesn't have a great way to do this. Sure you can record your mix, but that still requires 'performing' it one shot, in realtime, with no chance to fix mistakes or make tweaks later. I don't want to record the outputs; I want to record the inputs ("adjust this slider", "press this button", "nudge this jog wheel")... like the piano roll from a player piano. DJ software isn't built for this. So, what we need instead is a full DAW. Enter [Reaper](https://www.reaper.fm/). There are many DAWs, but Reaper is fantastic, shockingly affordable (and without a license, the gentlest of nagware), runs on linux, and is friendly to both the beginner and power-user. So how do we make a DJ set in Reaper? Well... I'm a rank amaetur in both DJing and digital music production, but I want to share a workflow that has worked well for me, and has been tons of fun. The general principle is: - Load the songs of your playlist into separate DAW tracks - Normalize their playback speed to all have the same BPM (i.e., beatmatch everything) - Arrange the tracks in time for desired mix-in/out points - Use the tracks' volume envelopes to cross-fade - Recover your original desired BPM throughout the set via the playrate envelope on Reaper's "master" track In reality, the full process has a few more steps: - [Track acquisition](#track-dl) - [Track selection and rough cut](#rough-cut) - [_Beatmatching_](#beatmatch) - [_Arranging_](#arranging) - [_Transitions_](#transitions) - [_BPM recovery_](#bpm-recovery) - [Mastering](#mastering) First, a caveat: a DAW is not a DJ controller; trying to replicate every bell and whistle thereof will be a cumbersome and frustrating experience. While Reaper is very powerful, and with enough effort probably _can_ simulate anything a DJ deck can do, that doesn't mean you should. A counter-caveat: **NO ONE CARES ABOUT YOUR DJ TRANSITIONS** Most of my friends DJ using a Spotify playlist with auto-crossfade and everyone still has a great time. Good track selection is 95% of a good mix. Transitions are 5%, and poring over fancy transitions that you aren't even doing live honestly feels a bit cringe. In my opinion, simpler is better (both for the end result and for your sanity), and a DAW does the 'simple' parts very very well. What can a DAW do well vs. not? - _stupid easy_ -- volume crossfade, BPM adjust, pitch adjust, loops, hot cues, samples - _reasonably easy_ -- EQ - _doable_ -- stem separation - _diminishing returns_ -- most FX: echo out, reverb, vinyl effects (scratch, brake, backspin) So why do this at all if you can't showboat your florid transitions? Well, simply, beatmatching is insanely fun. Personally I love the sound of a beatmatched transition; it keeps the energy high and feels like you're being propelled forward into the next track. Now, into the weeds... ## Step 1: Downloading Music MP3s are an endangered species these days, but in order to mix, you need local copies of all your audio files -- streaming services won't do. I can't remember the last time I couldn't find a track I wanted on youtube. With the [yt-dlp](https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp) command line tool, I can have that track on my hard drive in seconds. The `-x` flag will further extract just the audio. Be sure to install the latest version as these download bots and streaming sites are in a constant arms race. People act like if you download off youtube, your mix will sound so ass you'll have to go into exile. While you will likely be capped at ~128kbps quality and won't win any audiophile awards, in practice it hasn't bothered me. Another nebulous claim is that youtube is EQ'ed/squashed for home listening on crappy laptop speakers, and it will be painfully obvious only once you're on the club sound system. Well, it's hard to falsify that claim because I don't play on the club sound system. But our speakers aren't exactly small and again, it hasn't affected me in practice... There definitely are bad re-rips out there uploaded by gods-knows-who (a risk as old as Napster), but if you get from the official channel, I assume the quality will be sufficient, especially since youtube now runs its own music service too. If you're still concerned, you can buy the track off [Beatport](https://www.beatport.com/). ## Step 2: Track Selection, Rough Cut The earliest stages of making a set involves just messing around with tracks to see what sounds good with each other. For this, I would definitely recommend still getting proficient in a live DJ program (I prefer Mixxx), and doing most of your noodling there. It is software suited to the task, and makes a great playground for experimentation when you're still conceiving your set. Once you have a concept of your track ordering and the transitions you'd like to make, start codifying things in Reaper. ## Step 3: Beatmatching The Reaper beatgrid is what makes this whole thing work. Our goal is to align every track's beats to the beatgrid. Once that is done, we can move tracks around at will while staying... on beat. Note that this necessarily homogenizes all tracks to play at the same tempo. We fix this later during [BPM recovery](#bpm-recovery). Since we want the beatgrid to work for us, ensure 'Snap' is enabled in your Reaper workspace. This locks the cursor to exact beats. We will only disable it for specialized operations. Beatmatching is the most technical step, but once you get the hang of it, it can be done quite quickly. The procedure is thus: ### Scenario 1: Integer BPM - load your track into Mixxx (or equivalent) and note its BPM (pic of bpm in mixxx) - create a new track in Reaper and insert the track (Insert > Media file) - right-click on the content > Item properties... - Playback rate > Set... > Source BPM > enter the BPM from Mixxx - turn off 'Loop source' - (optionally, you can transpose the pitch/key here via 'Pitch Adjust') {:style="margin-bottom: 0;"} - align the first beat: - zoom way in on the first beat of the song (just the first obvious percussion or transient -- doesn't need to be the exact first beat) - align this beat to the beat grid via shift+drag (shift disables snap-to-grid) {:style="margin-bottom: 0;"} - now examine a beat near the end of the track and ensure it is still aligned to the beat grid - if so, examine a few other places elsewhere in the track to ensure beat alignment throughout Note we never actually listened to the song during this process-- it's much easier/faster to align the beats visually via the waveform, rather than by ear. But if you are struggling to ascertain the beats visually, add a click track (Insert > Click Source) to 'hear' the beat grid. Here's what this 'happy path' looks like: (vid of ride or die) ### Scenario 2: Fractional BPM If subsequent beats are not aligned, the BPM you used is not accurate enough. While EDM will typically have a very consistent and integer BPM, there are exceptions: - some music indeed has a non-whole-number BPM - BPM may waver slightly throughout the track (vinyl rips, older analog production) - the track truly has a variable tempo/BPM, in which case you have your work cut out for you (see: [Advanced Tempo](#tempo)) If you've doublechecked your BPM and grid alignment still fails, make fine adjustments via _stretch markers_. - disable grid snapping (alt+S) - place the cursor on the beat - create a stretch marker (shift+W) - re-enable grid snapping (alt+S) - drag the marker to the beatgrid This places the marked beat on the grid, while elastically stretching everything before and after (with the goal that all those other beats now also fall on the beatgrid). Here's a worked example with Daft Punk's _Harder, Better, Faster, Stronger_. (vid of HBFS) Daft Punk tracks are notorious for unusual BPMs. Let's naively trust the commonly cited BPM of 123 (rounded off from the true BPM of ~123.48). We align the first beat, but due to our imprecise starting BPM, the end of the track needs correction with a stretch marker. Easy enough. The first and last beats are now aligned, but as we scan the track we see the beatgrid is still wrong. The beat goes in and out of phase with the grid twice (a ["beat frequency"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beat_(acoustics)) ha ha), meaning our final beat is 2 beats off. Our beats are slightly too short (since the beat drift moves opposite to the direction of scan), so we lenghten by moving the stretch marker two beats later. Are we done yet? No. The stretch nudges all the beats on the track, but in order for those beats to now fall on the beatgrid, the stretch must be anchored at the right place. The anchoring defaults to the start/end of the track, which is not what we want: - For the start of the track, we want the stretch anchored at the first _beat_. By instead anchoring at the absolute start, we see our first beat has now been pulled slightly out of sync. We can anchor and correct the first beat with another stretch marker. (The difference is minimal, but why undo the work we did of aligning the first beat?) - For the end of the track, the tail segment after the last beat is now stretched severely. Unlike the start, the difference is _not_ minimal, due to the short time interval between the last beat's stretch marker and the end of the track. We want the tail to play back at its original speed (technically, at the slightly stretched speed the rest of the track now plays at, but the difference is negligible). Essentially, we don't want this tail stretched at all, but rather to 'float' after the stretch marker. There are several ways to accomplish this with varying degrees of _stretchmarker-fu_ -- but the easiest is to double-click on the stretch marker, which resets the stretch after the marker (or, you can achieve the desired floating behavior upfront by holding alt+shift when originally dragging the stretch marker). Note that this is a complex example, meant to show what happens when you start with a coarse BPM and are off by more than a full beat. A more accurate starting BPM avoids most of the headache. Mixxx reports a BPM of 123.5 for this track (and its detail view shows a full unrounded (though still not 100% accurate) BPM of 123.46779) -- close enough to get away with just slightly adjusting the final beat and avoiding all this ancillary clean-up. ### Scenario 3: Non-steady BPM If the beatgrid _still_ isn't aligned throughout, your track suffers from a non-constant BPM. Here's such a track whose BPM is not perfectly steady: (vid miko mission) The first and last beats _are_ aligned properly, but the beat drifts slightly and without a clear pattern, deviating up to ~1/5th of a beat. You can add more stretch markers to tame it (make sure you've anchored your first and last beats with their own markers first!) **HOWEVER**, it's important to not get too OCD about this. Humans struggle to resolve beat differences of less than 20 milliseconds (1/25th of a beat at 120 BPM). AND, precise beat alignment only really matters when you're mixing with another track. Don't slavishly mold the track to the beatgrid. Stretch markers shouldn't be about perceptably changing the pace of a song, but rather zeroing out accumulated error. If you're placing too many stretch markers too close together, you may be flattening out the 'groove' of the music that arises from imperfection. If needed, you can always add additional stretch markers to problem areas later. ### Beat-aligning the track start Whew, we're almost done -- the track's beats are all aligned to the beatgrid, but the start of the track is not. This means as soon as we move the track, the start will snap to a gridline, making all that follows now off-beat. To avoid this, we must extend the track start to also start on a beat. But let's take this further... (video of extending start, and moving to show snap) As you zoom out, grid snapping will occur on ever more coarse boundaries (every measure, every 4 measures, etc.). For more ergonomic snapping behavior, extend the start of track so that there's a full 4 bars (or multiple thereof) of lead-in before the first downbeat. When mixing music, you always want to sync on the downbeat (1st beat of a bar/measure), and even more practically, on the 4- or 8-bar intervals that delineate musical phrases. By keeping the track start in-line with these boundaries, the structure of all tracks will stay musically cohesive even as you move them around the timeline. Otherwise, every time you moved a track you'd find its downbeat out of sync from the other tracks', which is extremely irritating. Think of this as a higher nested level of syncing with the beatgrid. (If extending doesn't prepend the track with silence, you forgot to disable 'Loop source') (pic) Note: extending the track start seems to (at least on my version of Reaper) trigger an annoying bug where your beats appear to shift slightly off-grid. As far as I can tell, this is just an artifact of displaying the waveform. If you zoom way in, the beats are still aligned. ## Step 4: Arranging the Timeline, Mix-in/out Points Once all tracks are beatmatched, you can generally drag your tracks around at will and they won't sound like a trainwreck. More likely than not, they'll even sound good together. You'll probably already have your target mixing points from [Step 2](#rough-cut). Most dance music is structured around 8-bar phrases. If you move tracks in units of 8 bars, they generally stay sync with each other in a pleasing way. but sometimes aligning at 4-bar markers is appropriate too. sometimes a track sneakily extends/reduces a phrase by 1 or 2 bars, meaning you'll have to note the alignment change thereafter One place where a DAW really shines compared to linear mixing is how easily you can chop up and rearrange tracks. Often there's a track with a good hook, but it drags on for too long or gets repetitive. You can easily cut out just the parts you want ('S' for split). As long as you chop on the 8-bar boundaries, you likely won't even be able to tell something is missing. If there is a noticeable 'jump cut' from e.g., a choral effect, you can create a bit of overlap in the cut and reaper will auto-crossfade the overlapping items. You can also repeat segments of items for looping cut-pasted items still remember their offsets in the parents track so you can extend the start and end easily ripple editing as needed if making changes not at end i prefer to not keep this turned on all the time to minimize 'spooky action at a distance' ## Step 5: DJ Transitions! AKA the fun part. Let's start blending one track into another. ### Working with envelopes Almost all of these involve setting up an _envelope_ or 'automation lane' linked to the track. These are time-varying parameters defined by control points. First learn to manipulate these control points: - add a point -- shift+click - delete a point -- alt+click - select multiple points -- right-click+drag a selection region The y-axis on most envelopes is very compressed, where even a 1-pixel change makes a big difference. Avoid frustration and exercise precise control via: - move a point horizontally only, with vertical position locked -- ctrl+shift+drag - set the point's value directly -- right-click > Set point value... Also, this project setting [icon] controls whether track content and track envelopes are moved in lockstep. I.e., when you move a track on the timeline, does the envelope follow or stay put. I would suggest you keep this setting **on** (envelope follows). Now, onto different transition techniques. In rough order of usefulness for me personally: ### Crossfade The workhorse... 90% of transitions are a simple crossfade. Two volume envelopes -- one on each track -- gradually fade in the incoming track and fade out the outgoing track. Sometimes you can omit an envelope if the track suitably fades on its own. Create the volume envelope: on the track's left-side tab, click the 'trim' folder > toggle 'Volume' under 'Track Envelopes'. Note that if you're already adjusted the track's gain using the faders in the bottom pane, that gain gets baked into the envelope and the fader gets reset to 0db. I don't like this behavior so I undo it manually (when I remember to). Set the fade curve to your liking with control points. Personally I like a bit of hard cut on either end. Make sure that, out side of the transition zone, the envelope is set to normal volume (0db) or muted (−∞db). Ideally you don't want envelopes influencing the sound outside of defined transitions; it's easy to overlook later and makes it hard to debug why your mix is not sounding the way you expect. Don't be too precious about the exact fade curve here, as relative volumes may change during the [mastering phase](#mastering); you'll dial it in then. ### Hot Cue (Gate Cue) Alternative to a gradual fade is to tease/hype up the incoming track with some snippets of the baseline and then drop in the new track at full blast. I believe the closest equivalent to this in DJ world is using Hot Cues in Gated Playback mode. To do this, just copy the snippets you want from the incoming track and paste them at the appropriate lead-in points. Remember that snippets are just offsets into the parent tracks, so their start/stop points can be easily extended without having to copy a new snippet. If you want you can even take this technique farther and start doing quasi-mashups of the two tracks. ### Echo out If you want to end a track abruptly or without a good outro, use an echo effect to soften the landing. This is our first use of an FX plugin. - enable the plugin on the track: click the 'FX' button on the track's tab > search for 'ReaDelay' > Add - configure the plugin - set Volume to control decay rate (-3db is a good start) - the echo won't repeat unless you use Feedback (set it to match Volume) - set the echo delay, likely 1 beat or 1/2 beat. Specify in 'Length (musical)' in units of \\( \frac{1}{8} \\)th notes (1 beat = 1 quarter note = 2× \\( \frac{1}{8} \\)th notes) {:style="margin-bottom: 0;"} - we don't want the track to echo the whole time, so we need to activate it at a certain point via automation: - In the FX dialog, click 'Param' > FX Parameter List > Show Track Envelope > '1: Enabled' - this creates the envelope to automate turning on the echo at the right time - shape this envelope to start out as off, and switch on just before the track's mix-out point If your echo delay is 1 beat, you need to activate the effect at least 1 beat before the track end (otherwise you'll just be echoing silence). If you activate it exactly 1× delay units before end, the echo repitition sounds a bit abrupt, so I would suggest starting a bit earlier to prime the echo for longer. But this also means the audible echo starts earlier, which can be distracting. Should you care, you can gradually bring in the echo effect by automating and fading in the effect's 'Wet' param. (In FX parlance, 'Dry' refers to the original audio and 'Wet' is solely the audio added by the effect). In other words: 'Enabled' controls whether the currently playing audio is being fed into the echo; 'Wet' volume controls how much of that echo you can hear in the output. This is a nice example of how a DAW sometimes gives you too much flexibility, compared to a one-button operation on a DJ controller. ### EQ mixing EQ is also done via an FX plugin, ReaEQ. Lots of DJ tutorials online swear by EQ mixing. But remember that every adjustment you make is something you have to record for automation. If you diddle with 15 different knobs, that's a lot of envelopes to manage. I find the biggest bang-for-the-buck is to manage your EQ as a highpass filter, controlled by a single parameter -- the frequency cutoff. This let's you cut out the bass frequencies, which is the main EQ use case of avoiding 'bass clash'. To configure, add the plugin as in Echo Out. Remove all band tabs except for '1'. Set Type to 'High Pass'. Make the cutoff a bit steeper by setting Bandwidth to 1.5 Create an envelope for 'Freq-High Pass 1' The 'Wet' param again controls how much of the effect comes through, so if you're cutting out the bass but set Wet to 70%, 30% volume of the original bass will still come through. One concern is that, althought you can drop the frequency cutoff down to 20Hz, it doens't turn off entirely. And the cutoff is not particularly steep, so i'm worried about attenuating my low freqs at all times in way that my headphone can't tell but the subwoofer on d-day will. So out of paranoia, I add another envelope for 'Bypass' to engage the effect only during the transition. ### Tape Stop / Vinyl Brake Variable rate stretch marker but we have pitch lock turned on, so need to split item where slowdown starts, and turn off pitch lock. but now pitch will be wrong bc of playrate, need to compensate with pitch adjust A slight annoyance, playrate is expressed as a multiplier, while relative pitch is expressed in the musical unit of _semitones_ (the difference from C to C♯ -- 12 semitones in an octave). Convert between the two via $$ -12 \log_2 playrate $$. create a 2nd marker to have a period of stopped audio, to avoid blip. unfortunately, combined with playrate adjustment on the master track, this creates some artifact at the very slow end, which you might want to fade out can also not turn off pitch lock and play with pitch manually. less accurate but fun: take > pitch envelope, set a custom range I've never actually used this, wanted to challenge to see how it could be done. as should be obvious, attempting other 'vinyl' effects by hand is extremely laborious without a purpose-built FX plugin ### Other FX I feel I was reaching the point of diminishing returns here, trying to chase more advanced effects. Mainly by the effort-to-reward ratio, where I didn't feel much need for them in my mixes so any reward would be low. But Reaper has tons of plugins (both built-in and 3rd party) that, with enough work, can probably set up anything you want. As I said before, just because you can doesn't necessarily mean you should. But on the other hand, creativity for its own sake is fun, so by all means, explore. ## Step 6: BPM Recovery We likely want our tracks to play at something resembling their original speed. Currently they're all a single flat BPM from beatmatching. global playrate enable constant pitch master track, playrate envelope to control output bpm. not a lot of resolution. 1.##x = eg 1.15 * base bpm of 120 = 138bpm can edit directly in RPP file in text editor if need exact instantaneous bpm changes are a bit tricky. how you handle depends on exactly what you want to achieve and how tolerant you are instantaneous playrate change – will cause audio artifact tempo change and bpm rebase deep dive on bpm rate change exponential vs linear I went down a bit of a rabbithole after noticing that the rate the BPM is changing is set by your forward progression through the mix, which itself is set by the BPM, which is changing in response to BPM… The playrate envelope gives the appearance of a nice constant change to BPM, but I realized there's a wrinkle. How fast the BPM changes is linked to your speed through the track. But… as your BPM increases, you move through the track faster, which means the BPM increases faster, so you move through the track faster… seemingly giving you this runaway effect. I wanted to characterize this.
Math! Let us define our location within the mix as position \\(p\\), in units of beats. Our playback speed is \\( v= \frac{dp}{dt} \\), i.e., \\( \frac{beats}{minute} \\). We have a start BPM \\(v_0\\), a target BPM \\( (v_0 + \Delta v) \\), and an interval of beats over which to complete the transition \\(\Delta p\\). The playrate envelope in reaper will change the BPM \\(v\\) at a fixed slope with respect to position \\(p\\): \\( v(p) = v_0 + \tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta p} p \\) To get BPM with respect to elapsed time \\(t\\) (in minutes), we must solve the differential equation \\( p'(t) = v_0 + \tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta p} p \\) with initial condition \\( p(0) = 0 \\), yielding: $$ p(t) = \frac{v_0}{\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta p}} (e^{\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta p} t}-1) $$ $$ v(t) = v_0 e^{\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta p} t} $$ We can see there is indeed an exponential runaway term. However, this is not how a human DJ would adjust the BPM live. They would move the BPM slider at a steady rate in the time domain, i.e.: \\( v(t) = v_0 + \tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} t \\) This equation is not self-referential, so we can get position via simple integration: \\( p(t) = \int v(t) \,dt = v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} t^2 \\). Note we must solve for \\(\Delta t\\): given \\( p(\Delta t) = \Delta p \\), \\( \Delta t = \frac{\Delta p}{v_0 + \frac{1}{2}\Delta v} \\) To backport this constant-via-time BPM change into the reaper envelope, we must redefine \\( v(t) \\) with respect to \\(p\\). First we must get \\( t(p) \\) from \\( p(t) \\) via the quadratic equation \\( At^2 + Bt + C = 0 \\), where \\( A = \tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} \\), \\( B = v_0 \\), \\( C = -p \\). $$ t(p) = \frac{-v_0 + \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}p}}{\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}} $$ Substituting \\( t(p) \\) into \\( v(t) \\): $$ \begin{aligned} v(p) &= \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2\tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}p} \\ &= \sqrt{v_0^2 + \frac{2v_0 \Delta v + \Delta v^2}{\Delta p} p} \end{aligned} $$ [ show graph comparing envelopes ] Does this difference matter in practice? [show graph p vs t and v vs t] Apparently not. I'd say it's inconsequential for any sane BPM transition surprised that the relative differences are time invariant We tend to perceive changes as relative difference, so let's analyze how fast the BPM is changing at any moment. What does that look like? We need to compute acceleration \\(a\\) ( \\( \frac{BPM}{minute} \\) i.e., \\( \frac{beats}{min \cdot min} \\) ) with respect to \\(v\\). Reaper method (linear w.r.t. position): \\( a(v) = \tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta p}v \\) (constant relative rate of change to BPM) DJ method (linear w.r.t. time): \\( a = \tfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} \\) (constant absolute rate of change to BPM) perceived change will be greater when BPM is lower could actually argue that exponential is more “correct” perceptually, and perhaps this is even what a human DJ would do subconsciously
